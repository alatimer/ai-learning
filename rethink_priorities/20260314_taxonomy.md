# Defence-in-Depth Taxonomy: AI Safety Subareas

*Compiled 2026-03-14. Organized by the three-layer defence framework from BlueDot Impact's AGI Strategy course (Unit 4), extended with a "Layer 0" meta-strategic layer. Each layer contains subareas, and each subarea lists specific technologies, techniques, and research directions.*

---

## Layer 0: Strategic Intelligence and Prioritization

*Goal: Understand the AI capability landscape, identify gaps in the safety ecosystem, and allocate effort across Layers 1–3 where it will have the highest marginal return. This is the prerequisite layer — without it, defensive efforts may be misallocated or duplicated.*

### 0.1 AI Capabilities Forecasting and Trajectory Analysis

Predicting the pace, direction, and nature of AI progress to inform when and where defences are needed.

- **AI timeline forecasting**: Estimating when transformative AI / AGI / ASI milestones will be reached. Epoch AI's compute trend analyses, AI Impacts expert surveys, Metaculus forecasting questions
- **Capability elicitation and benchmarking**: Systematically measuring what frontier models can do today and extrapolating trends. BIG-Bench, MMLU, ARC-AGI, METR task suites
- **Compute scaling analysis**: Modelling the relationship between compute, data, and model capabilities (scaling laws research). Epoch AI's training compute database
- **Algorithmic progress tracking**: Measuring how much less compute is needed over time to achieve the same capability level. Epoch AI's algorithmic efficiency research
- **Discontinuity and takeoff speed analysis**: Research on whether AI progress will be gradual or sudden, and implications for defence preparedness. AI Impacts' discontinuity database
- **Scenario modelling**: Developing concrete scenarios for how transformative AI development could unfold, to stress-test defensive strategies

### 0.2 Ecosystem Mapping and Gap Identification

Understanding who is working on what, where effort is concentrated, and where critical gaps exist.

- **Research landscape mapping**: Cataloging active research groups, their focus areas, and output. Larks' annual AI alignment literature reviews, AISafety.com field map
- **Funding landscape analysis**: Tracking where money flows across safety subareas. Open Philanthropy/Coefficient Giving database, LTFF payout reports, SFF grants
- **Talent mapping**: Estimating researcher headcounts per subarea to identify understaffed areas. 80,000 Hours estimates, AI safety career surveys
- **Neglectedness assessment**: Systematically comparing subareas by the INT (Importance, Neglectedness, Tractability) framework. 80,000 Hours problem profiles, Rethink Priorities research
- **Risk-adjusted gap analysis**: Identifying subareas where the gap between current effort and needed effort is largest given the risk, not just where absolute effort is lowest

### 0.3 Project Incubation and Field-Building

Growing the capacity of the AI safety ecosystem — people, organizations, and infrastructure.

- **AI safety training programs**: BlueDot Impact courses, MATS (ML Alignment Theory Scholars), ARENA, AI Safety Camp, Redwood Research REMIX
- **Career guidance and pipeline development**: 80,000 Hours advising, AI safety career fairs, university course development
- **Organization incubation**: Supporting new safety-focused organizations from founding to sustainability. Charity Entrepreneurship AI safety track, SFF/LTFF early-stage grants
- **Community infrastructure**: Alignment Forum, LessWrong, EA Forum as knowledge-sharing platforms; AI safety conferences and workshops (e.g., SafeAI at AAAI)
- **Bridging technical and policy communities**: Programs that connect technical AI safety researchers with policymakers (GovAI fellowship, CSET, RAND)

### 0.4 Portfolio Prioritization and Resource Allocation

Deciding how to distribute finite resources (funding, talent, attention) across the full defensive portfolio.

- **Cause prioritization research**: Comparing AI safety subareas against each other and against non-AI existential risks. Open Philanthropy worldview investigations, GiveWell/Rethink Priorities cost-effectiveness analyses
- **Theory of change development**: For each subarea, articulating how additional effort translates into reduced risk. Moving beyond intuition to structured models
- **Marginal value analysis**: Estimating the expected risk reduction per additional dollar or researcher in each subarea, accounting for diminishing returns in crowded areas
- **Funder coordination**: Reducing duplication and filling gaps across the major funders (Open Phil, SFF, LTFF, government). Currently mostly informal

### 0.5 Monitoring, Early Warning, and Progress Tracking

Continuously assessing whether safety is keeping pace with capabilities, and whether the defensive portfolio needs rebalancing.

- **Safety-capabilities parity tracking**: Measuring whether safety research progress is keeping up with capabilities progress. No standardized metric exists yet
- **Frontier lab monitoring**: Tracking what the leading labs are training, deploying, and publishing. Model cards, safety reports, third-party evaluations
- **Policy implementation tracking**: Monitoring whether regulations (EU AI Act, EO 14110, etc.) are being implemented effectively. OECD AI Policy Observatory
- **Incident monitoring**: Tracking real-world AI failures and near-misses. AI Incident Database (AIID), OECD AI Incidents Monitor
- **Prediction markets and forecasting platforms**: Aggregating informed opinion on AI timelines, capability milestones, and safety-relevant questions. Metaculus, Manifold Markets, Polymarket. Used by researchers and funders to inform prioritization decisions
- **Periodic reassessment**: Regular (e.g., annual) re-evaluation of the full defensive portfolio against updated threat models and capability forecasts

### 0.6 Coordination Infrastructure

Enabling information sharing and collective action across the fragmented AI safety ecosystem.

- **Inter-lab safety coordination**: Information sharing between frontier labs on safety-relevant findings (Frontier Model Forum, pre-deployment safety testing coordination)
- **Government-lab coordination**: Structured relationships for safety evaluation access (UK/US AISI model evaluation agreements)
- **International safety research coordination**: Networks connecting safety researchers across countries and institutions. GPAI, AI safety summits
- **Shared tooling and infrastructure**: Common evaluation frameworks, benchmark suites, and red-teaming tools that multiple organizations can use
- **Whistleblowing and information channels**: Protected channels for reporting safety concerns at AI labs (currently underdeveloped — see the 2024 open letter from current/former lab employees)

## Layer 1: Prevent Dangerous AI Training

*Goal: Stop dangerous AI systems from being built in the first place, through governance, controls, and international coordination.*

### 1.1 Compute Governance

The regulation and monitoring of computational resources needed for frontier AI development. Compute is the most measurable and controllable input to AI training.

- **Export controls on AI chips**: U.S. restrictions on advanced semiconductor exports (Oct 2022, updated Oct 2023); allied coordination on chip access (e.g., "Chips for Peace")
- **Compute monitoring and tracking**: Detecting large-scale training runs via energy consumption, chip procurement, or cloud usage patterns. Research by Lennart Heim (RAND/GovAI), Yonadav Sharvit
- **Compute thresholds for regulation**: Using FLOP thresholds to trigger safety requirements (EU AI Act's 10^25 FLOP threshold for GPAI with systemic risk; EO 14110's 10^26 FLOP reporting threshold)
- **Hardware-enabled governance**: On-chip mechanisms for licensing, monitoring, or restricting use of high-end AI accelerators. Very early-stage research
- **Cloud compute KYC (Know Your Customer)**: Identity verification and use-case screening for large cloud compute purchases
- **Public compute access / equity**: National AI Research Resource (NAIRR) in the US, EuroHPC — ensuring compute governance doesn't simply entrench incumbents

### 1.2 International Agreements and Treaties

Multilateral frameworks to prevent any actor from training civilisation-threatening AI systems.

- **International AI Treaty proposals**: Proposals to cap computational power for model training and establish collaborative safety infrastructure (e.g., the aitreaty.org open letter, 2024)
- **IAEA-for-AI model**: An international inspection and monitoring body for AI, analogous to nuclear energy oversight. Discussed by multiple governance researchers
- **CERN-for-AI model**: An international collaborative AI safety research laboratory where frontier research is conducted jointly rather than competitively
- **Bletchley / Seoul / Paris AI Safety Summits**: International declarations on AI safety cooperation (Bletchley Declaration 2023, Seoul 2024, Paris 2025)
- **Global Partnership on AI (GPAI)**: OECD-affiliated international initiative for AI governance cooperation
- **ASI deterrence frameworks**: Analysis of strategic dynamics around superintelligence development; analogies to nuclear deterrence. Research by Oscar Delaney and others
- **Prevention of premature superintelligence creation**: Proposals for international agreements specifically targeting ASI (Shulman & others, arxiv 2511.10783)

### 1.3 Government-Led AI Development

Proposals to centralize frontier AI development within state-backed or internationally coordinated projects.

- **"The Project" (Aschenbrenner)**: Government-led AGI development with military-grade security, drawing on Manhattan Project / Apollo analogies
- **Nationalization / public control of frontier AI**: Proposals for government ownership or control of the most capable AI systems
- **"Beyond a Manhattan Project for AGI" (RAND, 2025)**: Analysis of whether and how government-led approaches could work
- **Concentrated vs. distributed development debate**: Tension between security benefits of concentration and power-concentration risks. Research by Forethought Foundation ("Should There Be Just One Western AGI Project?")

### 1.4 Domestic AI Regulation and Policy

National-level regulatory frameworks, licensing regimes, and policy levers.

- **EU AI Act (2024)**: Risk-based regulatory framework with tiered requirements for high-risk systems and GPAI with systemic risk
- **U.S. Executive Order 14110 (Oct 2023)**: Required NIST safety standards, red-teaming, dual-use model reporting, and agency-level risk assessments
- **AI policy levers review (GovAI)**: Comprehensive taxonomy of U.S. government tools to shape AI R&D and deployment
- **Licensing regimes for frontier AI**: Proposals requiring licenses to train models above certain capability/compute thresholds
- **"Born secret" classification**: Making certain algorithmic innovations classified at creation to prevent proliferation — modeled on nuclear weapons information controls
- **Pre-deployment safety demonstration requirements**: Mandating affirmative proof of safety before deploying frontier models (distinct from absence of detected flaws)
- **Liability regimes**: Legal liability for AI developers (strict liability, negligence standards) as incentive for safety investment

### 1.5 Safe-by-Design AI Architectures

Designing AI systems that are inherently less dangerous than general-purpose autonomous agents.

- **Scientist AI / tool AI**: Non-agentic AI systems designed for understanding and analysis rather than autonomous action. Reduces risk of power-seeking behavior (arxiv 2502.15657)
- **Myopic / bounded AI**: Limiting models' planning horizons and optimization scope to reduce instrumental convergence toward dangerous goals
- **Capability restrictions at the architecture level**: Designing models without certain capabilities (e.g., autonomous web access, self-replication) from the ground up rather than adding post-hoc restrictions

### 1.6 Safety Culture and Race Dynamics

Addressing competitive pressures that undermine safety in AI development.

- **Voluntary safety commitments**: Frontier AI Safety Commitments (Seoul 2024) on safety testing, red-teaming, and incident reporting
- **Industry self-regulation**: Frontier Model Forum and similar industry bodies setting voluntary safety standards
- **Race dynamics mitigation**: Research on institutional designs that reduce competitive pressure to cut safety corners (GovAI, CAIS)
- **Safety research allocation norms**: Establishing expectations for what fraction of resources frontier labs devote to safety vs. capabilities
- **Responsible scaling policies**: Lab-specific frameworks tying deployment decisions to safety evaluation results (Anthropic RSP, DeepMind Frontier Safety Framework, OpenAI Preparedness Framework)

## Layer 2: Constrain Dangerous AI Capabilities

*Goal: Once an AI system is trained, ensure it remains under control and cannot take catastrophic actions. Covers alignment, control, evaluation, and security.*

### 2.1 Technical Alignment Research

Ensuring AI systems reliably pursue intended objectives and serve human interests.

- **RLHF (Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback)**: Training models to follow human preferences via reward models learned from human comparisons. Core technique at Anthropic, OpenAI, DeepMind
- **Constitutional AI (CAI)**: Anthropic's approach using AI-generated feedback based on explicit principles ("constitution") to train harmless and helpful models
- **DPO (Direct Preference Optimization)**: Simplified alternative to RLHF that directly optimizes for human preferences without a separate reward model
- **Reward modeling**: Learning proxy reward functions from human feedback; research on avoiding Goodhart's Law / reward hacking / specification gaming
- **Value learning**: Broader program of learning human values from behavior, preferences, and stated principles. Includes inverse reward design, cooperative inverse reinforcement learning (CIRL)
- **Outer alignment**: Correctly specifying the training objective so that the "intended" goal matches what we actually want
- **Inner alignment / mesa-optimization**: Ensuring learned models actually pursue the training objective internally, rather than developing misaligned mesa-objectives
- **Goal stability and drift**: Preventing AI objectives from changing over time through adaptation or deployment in new contexts
- **Corrigibility / power aversion**: Training models to be correctable, interruptible, and to avoid seeking power as an instrumental goal
- **Pluralistic alignment**: Aligning AI with diverse human values rather than a single value system; handling moral uncertainty

### 2.2 Scalable Oversight

Maintaining human oversight as AI systems become more capable than humans in specific domains.

- **Debate**: Two AI systems argue opposing positions for a human judge, making complex reasoning easier to evaluate (Irving et al., 2018)
- **Recursive reward modeling**: Decomposing oversight tasks recursively so humans only need to evaluate simpler sub-tasks
- **Task decomposition / factored cognition**: Breaking complex tasks into smaller pieces that humans can verify independently (Ought's Elicit)
- **Iterated amplification**: Training AI assistants by bootstrapping from human oversight of simpler tasks
- **AI-assisted evaluation**: Using AI systems to help humans evaluate other AI systems' outputs
- **Eliciting Latent Knowledge (ELK)**: ARC's research agenda on extracting truthful internal knowledge from models that may have learned to deceive (Paul Christiano et al.)
- **Sandwiching**: Using AI to help non-expert humans evaluate expert-level AI outputs (Anthropic research)

### 2.3 Mechanistic Interpretability

Understanding the internal computations of AI models at the level of circuits and features.

- **Sparse autoencoders**: Decomposing model activations into interpretable features. Major recent progress (Anthropic, OpenAI, DeepMind)
- **Circuit-level analysis**: Identifying specific computational circuits responsible for particular capabilities (Anthropic's Transformer Circuits thread)
- **Feature visualization**: Understanding what model neurons/features represent
- **Probing / representation analysis**: Using simple classifiers to understand what information is encoded in model representations
- **Causal tracing / activation patching**: Identifying which components causally contribute to specific model behaviors
- **Superposition and polysemanticity**: Understanding how models encode more features than they have dimensions, and disentangling them
- **Scaling interpretability**: Making interpretability techniques work on frontier-scale models (billions of parameters). Current key challenge
- **Deceptive alignment detection via interpretability**: Using mechanistic understanding to detect whether models are genuinely aligned or merely appearing so

### 2.4 AI Control

Constraining AI behavior through external mechanisms rather than relying on genuine alignment — defense in depth for when alignment is uncertain.

- **Monitoring and tripwires**: Runtime monitoring systems that detect and flag suspicious AI behavior
- **Sandboxing and containment**: Restricting AI systems' access to external resources, tools, and communication channels
- **Capability elicitation testing**: Probing what a model can do (even if it doesn't spontaneously do it) to understand risk surface
- **Trusted monitoring**: Using trusted (smaller/weaker) AI systems to monitor untrusted (more capable) systems. Research by Redwood Research, Buck Shlegeris
- **Multi-agent oversight**: Multiple AI systems cross-checking each other's outputs
- **Honeypots and canaries**: Deliberately planted opportunities for misalignment to reveal deceptive models
- **Limiting autonomy and scope**: Graduated deployment with human checkpoints; constraining the action space of AI agents

### 2.5 AI Safety Evaluations and Red-Teaming

Testing and measuring the safety properties of AI systems before and after deployment.

- **Dangerous capability evaluations**: Testing models for capabilities that could enable catastrophic harm (bioweapons knowledge, cyberoffense, deception, self-replication). METR, UK/US AISIs, Apollo Research
- **Alignment evaluations**: Testing whether models exhibit aligned behavior under various conditions, including adversarial ones
- **Scheming / deception evaluations**: Specifically testing for strategic deception, sycophancy, and alignment faking. Apollo Research's scheming reasoning evaluations
- **Red-teaming**: Adversarial testing by human teams to find failure modes and jailbreaks. Standard practice at frontier labs
- **Automated red-teaming**: Using AI to generate adversarial inputs at scale
- **Jailbreak research**: Studying techniques that circumvent safety guardrails (e.g., Pliny the Liberator's L1B3RT4S database); developing defenses (constitutional classifiers)
- **Benchmark development**: Creating standardized safety benchmarks. MLCommons AI Safety Benchmark, HELM, etc.
- **Affirmative safety evidence**: Moving from "no detected flaws" to positive demonstration of safety properties. Proposed by CAIS

### 2.6 Training Data Safety

Controlling what models learn by curating or filtering training data.

- **Input data filtration**: Removing content from training data that could teach dangerous capabilities (e.g., bioweapons synthesis instructions, cyberattack tools)
- **Pretraining data filtering (Anthropic)**: Systematic filtering of harmful content during initial training phases to prevent capability acquisition
- **Unlearning**: Post-training techniques to remove specific dangerous knowledge or capabilities from models
- **Data poisoning / backdoor detection**: Detecting and removing Trojan attacks or backdoors injected via training data

### 2.7 Model Weight Security

Preventing theft, leakage, or unauthorized access to trained model weights.

- **RAND five-level security framework**: Graduated security tiers from protection against amateur hackers (Level 1) to nation-state adversaries (Level 5)
- **Secure enclaves and confidential computing**: Hardware-level protections for model weights during training and inference
- **Access control and compartmentalization**: Limiting who within an organization can access full model weights
- **Insider threat protection**: Preventing theft or sabotage by employees or contractors
- **Supply chain security for AI hardware**: Securing the hardware and software stack used for training against tampering

### 2.8 Robustness

Ensuring AI systems behave reliably under adversarial conditions and distributional shift.

- **Adversarial robustness**: Defending against adversarial examples designed to cause misclassification or undesired behavior. Adversarial training, certified defenses
- **Out-of-distribution robustness**: Maintaining performance when inputs differ from training data. Benchmarks: ImageNet-C, ImageNet-R, WILDS
- **Black swan / tail risk robustness**: Handling novel, unprecedented situations gracefully rather than catastrophically
- **Anomaly / out-of-distribution detection**: Identifying when inputs are outside the training distribution and flagging uncertainty

### 2.9 Honesty and Calibration

Ensuring AI systems are truthful, well-calibrated, and transparent about their uncertainty.

- **Calibration**: Model confidence matching actual accuracy
- **Selective prediction / knowing when to abstain**: Models declining to answer when they are likely to be wrong
- **Reducing hallucination and sycophancy**: Preventing models from generating plausible-sounding but false information, or agreeing with users regardless of truth
- **Epistemic humility**: Models accurately representing the limits of their knowledge

## Layer 3: Withstand Dangerous AI Actions

*Goal: Build societal resilience so that even if dangerous AI systems are deployed or escape containment, the damage is survivable and recoverable.*

### 3.1 Biosecurity Defenses

Protecting against AI-enabled biological threats, particularly engineered pandemics.

- **Nucleic acid synthesis screening**: Automated screening of gene synthesis orders against pathogen databases. Key bottleneck for AI-enabled bioweapons
  - SecureDNA (Kevin Esvelt, MIT): cryptographic screening system for synthesis providers
  - International Gene Synthesis Consortium (IGSC): industry self-regulation covering ~80% of commercial synthesis
  - EO 14110 requirements for NIST/OSTP screening standards
- **Metagenomic sequencing and pathogen surveillance**: Sequencing environmental samples to detect novel pathogens before clinical spread
  - Nucleic Acid Observatory (NAO, MIT): untargeted metagenomic sequencing of wastewater; goal of detecting any novel pathogen at ~1% population infection
  - CDC National Wastewater Surveillance System
  - Biobot Analytics, Ginkgo Bioworks biosurveillance unit
  - AI-powered anomaly detection for unusual genomic signatures
- **Pandemic preparedness infrastructure**:
  - Platform vaccines: mRNA and protein nanoparticle platforms for rapid adaptation (CEPI 100-day vaccine goal)
  - Broad-spectrum antivirals effective across pathogen families
  - Far-UVC germicidal lighting (222nm), improved indoor air quality (HEPA, CR boxes)
  - BARDA/ARPA-H funding for biodefense countermeasures
- **Access controls for biology**:
  - Know-your-customer (KYC) for lab equipment and biological agent purchases
  - BSL-3/BSL-4 laboratory security strengthening
  - Enhanced dual-use research of concern (DURC) oversight, extended to AI-generated biological insights
- **AI biosecurity-specific research**: Studies on whether LLMs provide meaningful "uplift" for bioweapon development (RAND 2024 study, Anthropic/OpenAI red-teaming). Informs defence priorities

### 3.2 Cybersecurity Defenses

Hardening digital infrastructure against AI-enhanced cyberattacks.

- **Critical infrastructure protection**:
  - CISA programs for AI-enabled threat defence
  - ICS/SCADA security: air-gapping, network segmentation, anomaly detection for power grids, water treatment, transportation
  - Zero-trust architecture (NIST SP 800-207)
  - Software supply chain security: SBOM mandates, SLSA framework, Sigstore code signing
- **AI-enhanced cyber defence**:
  - AI-powered threat detection: ML-based network traffic analysis, EDR, SIEM/SOAR (CrowdStrike, Darktrace, SentinelOne, Microsoft Security Copilot)
  - Automated vulnerability discovery and patching: AI-assisted fuzzing (Google OSS-Fuzz), formal verification
  - LLM-assisted code review for vulnerability identification (GitHub Copilot security, Semgrep AI)
  - DARPA AI Cyber Challenge (AIxCC): building AI systems that autonomously find and fix software vulnerabilities
- **AI-specific cyber threats**:
  - Prompt injection / indirect prompt injection defences (OWASP Top 10 for LLM Applications)
  - Model poisoning and data poisoning defences
  - Autonomous cyber agent containment (emerging research area)
- **Resilient systems architecture**:
  - Redundancy and graceful degradation of critical systems
  - Post-quantum cryptography (NIST PQC standards: CRYSTALS-Kyber, CRYSTALS-Dilithium)
  - Distributed and decentralized infrastructure reducing single points of failure

### 3.3 Democratic and Information Resilience

Protecting democratic institutions and the information ecosystem from AI-enabled manipulation.

- **Deepfake detection and media authentication**:
  - Content provenance / C2PA standard: cryptographic signing of media at capture (Adobe, Microsoft, BBC, Nikon)
  - Deepfake detection models: Microsoft Video Authenticator, Intel FakeCatcher, FaceForensics++
  - AI-generated content watermarking: Google SynthID, Meta stable signature watermarking
  - Provenance-based approaches (considered more robust than detection)
- **Information integrity and misinformation defence**:
  - Prebunking / psychological inoculation: training people to recognize manipulation techniques (van der Linden, Cambridge; Google Jigsaw)
  - Platform integrity: AI-assisted content moderation, coordinated inauthentic behavior detection
  - Fact-checking infrastructure: IFCN-certified fact-checkers, AI-assisted claim verification
  - Media literacy programs (Finland model)
  - Collective intelligence: Community Notes, collaborative verification
- **Election security**:
  - AI-specific election threats: synthetic candidate impersonation, micro-targeted persuasion, AI voter suppression
  - Risk-limiting audits, voter-verified paper trails (CISA/EAC standards)
  - AI disclosure laws for campaign content (California AB 2655, EU AI Act synthetic media requirements)
  - Rapid response protocols for AI-generated election disinformation
- **Institutional and epistemic resilience**:
  - Research on the "liar's dividend" (real content dismissed as AI-generated)
  - AI-assisted deliberative democracy (Polis/vTaiwan, Anthropic collective constitutional AI)
  - Government AI capacity building: UK AISI, US AISI (NIST), EU AI Office

### 3.4 Economic Resilience

Adapting economic systems to withstand AI-driven disruption, particularly mass automation.

- **Labor market adaptation**:
  - AI labor displacement research (Frey & Osborne, OpenAI/UPenn GPT-exposure study, IMF 2024 40% job exposure estimate)
  - Reskilling and upskilling programs; evidence base on what interventions actually work (thin)
  - Workforce transition policy: TAA model adapted for AI displacement; EU Just Transition Fund precedent
- **Income and wealth distribution**:
  - Universal Basic Income (UBI): pilots in Finland, Stockton, Kenya, Sam Altman's OpenResearch study
  - Universal Basic Services: free provision of essentials instead of cash transfers
  - Windfall profits taxation on AI-generated surplus (Acemoglu's "so-so automation" work)
  - Broad-based ownership models: sovereign wealth funds from AI taxation, employee ownership, "AI dividend" proposals
- **Economic concentration and competition**:
  - Antitrust for AI: preventing monopolistic concentration of capabilities and data (FTC, EU DMA)
  - Open-source AI ecosystem: Meta Llama, Mistral, open-weight movement as competitive check
  - Public compute access: NAIRR (US), EuroHPC — preventing compute concentration as a permanent barrier
- **Long-run economic transformation**:
  - Post-scarcity economics (very underdeveloped)
  - Research on meaning and purpose beyond employment

### 3.5 AI Emergency Preparedness and Incident Response

Government and institutional readiness for AI-related crises.

- **Government response frameworks**:
  - NIST AI Risk Management Framework (AI RMF 1.0)
  - EO 14110 requirements for agency-level risk assessments
  - UK AI Safety Institute: pre-deployment evaluations of frontier models
  - US AI Safety Institute (NIST): developing evaluation standards
  - EU AI Act implementation and enforcement
- **AI incident response**:
  - AI Incident Database (AIID, Partnership on AI): 600+ cataloged real-world AI failures
  - OECD AI Incidents Monitor
  - "NTSB for AI" proposals: creating an independent body to investigate AI incidents (no equivalent institution yet exists)
  - Model recall / capability restriction mechanisms (mostly ad hoc currently)
  - Kill switch / emergency shutdown mechanisms (highly underdeveloped)
- **International coordination**:
  - Bletchley Declaration (2023), Seoul (2024), Paris (2025) summit series
  - International AI Safety Report (Bengio-led, 2025): IPCC-style expert assessment
  - UK-US AISI mutual evaluation agreements for frontier model access
  - Frontier AI Safety Commitments (Seoul 2024): voluntary company commitments
- **Tabletop exercises and scenario planning**:
  - RAND AI scenario exercises (nuclear C2 with AI, AI-enabled biothreats)
  - CISA tabletop exercises on AI-enabled election interference and infrastructure attacks
  - GovAI/CAIS catastrophic scenario research

### 3.6 Defensive Acceleration and Differential Technology Development

Strategic frameworks for ensuring defensive technologies outpace offensive ones.

- **Differential Technology Development (DTD)**: Bostrom's principle of preferentially accelerating protective technologies over destructive ones
- **d/acc (Vitalik Buterin)**: Distributed, decentralized AI access combined with robust defensive investment. Favors defence over offence across biosecurity, cybersecurity, and information integrity
- **def/acc**: Defensive acceleration — prioritizing defensive technology advancement specifically
- **Offense-defence balance analysis**: For each AI capability domain, analyzing whether it favours attackers or defenders and prioritizing defensive applications. Research at GovAI, RAND, CSET
- **Privacy-enhancing technologies**: Homomorphic encryption, secure multi-party computation, differential privacy — reduce harm from data breaches and surveillance
- **Decentralized infrastructure**: Blockchain-based identity, distributed systems reducing single points of failure
- **Physical-world resilience**: Distributed energy, resilient supply chains, local/additive manufacturing

## Cross-Cutting Observations

### Subareas that span multiple layers
- **Compute governance** (Layer 1 prevention + Layer 2 monitoring + Layer 3 enforcement)
- **AI safety evaluations** (Layer 1 pre-training assessment + Layer 2 post-training testing)
- **International coordination** (Layer 1 treaties + Layer 3 incident response)
- **Ecosystem mapping** (Layer 0 intelligence feeds into prioritization across all layers)

### Apparent gaps and underdeveloped areas
- **Layer 0**: Safety-capabilities parity metrics (no standardized way to measure whether safety is keeping pace); marginal value analysis across subareas; funder coordination beyond informal channels; whistleblowing protections at AI labs
- **Layer 1**: Hardware-enabled governance mechanisms; "born secret" classification for algorithms; formal frameworks for when to halt/restrict training runs
- **Layer 2**: Scaling interpretability to frontier models; rigorous metrics for alignment (vs. "no detected flaws"); ELK and latent knowledge extraction
- **Layer 3**: "NTSB for AI" incident investigation body; emergency shutdown mechanisms; post-scarcity economics; offense-defence balance measurement; AI labour transition programs with evidence-based design

### Where the layers interact
Layer 0 (strategic intelligence) determines how effort is allocated across Layers 1–3. Without it, defensive investment is driven by intuition, hype, or inertia rather than evidence. Layer 1 failure (a dangerous model is trained) increases dependence on Layer 2 (constraining it). Layer 2 failure (the model escapes constraints or is deployed unsafely) increases dependence on Layer 3 (society surviving the consequences). The taxonomy is designed to show that no single layer is sufficient — the strategic question is which subareas across all layers offer the highest marginal return on additional effort.
